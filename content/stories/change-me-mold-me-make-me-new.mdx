# Change Me, Mold Me

## The Song

There is a particular kind of discomfort that arrives when you stop making excuses. Not the guilt that comes from being caught, but the slower, heavier recognition that something in you needs to change — and that you have known it for longer than you want to admit. This song came from that place. A season of honest self-examination that was less dramatic than it sounds and more grinding than anyone tends to describe. No crisis moment. Just the accumulated weight of personal failure sitting across the table, asking to be dealt with.

The desire for change was real. That part was not the problem. The problem was what genuine change seemed to require — the willingness to have something taken from you, reshaped, handed back in a form you did not entirely choose. Surrender sounds clean in a lyric. In practice, it feels more like reluctance. The song was written inside that reluctance, not beyond it. It does not arrive at resolution because, at the time of writing, resolution had not arrived.

What remained, when the defensiveness cleared, was a prayer. Not a polished one. The kind you say when you have run out of alternatives — when you mean it precisely because you cannot quite afford not to. "Change me, mold me" is that prayer. It is spoken to God not as a formality but as an admission: that the speaker cannot accomplish what needs to be accomplished alone, and that the hands doing the shaping are not their own.

## The Sound

The arrangement was built to stay out of the way. Soft, steady rhythms that do not press. Harmonic movement that unfolds slowly, without urgency or resolution-chasing. The production was deliberately uncluttered because the lyric needed room to breathe, and because the subject itself — genuine transformation — is not a thing that happens quickly. The music does not rush toward a climax. It holds a posture.

The melodic repetition throughout the song is intentional. Spiritual growth is not linear or varied or interesting in the ways we might prefer. It tends to return to the same ground, again and again, with small differences accumulating over time. The repetition in the arrangement mirrors that honestly. This is not a song about a moment of change. It is a song about the ongoing process of being changed — which is quieter, longer, and less cinematic than most worship music allows.

## The Scripture

Two texts sit beneath this song. The first is from the book of Jeremiah, chapter eighteen. God tells the prophet to go to a potter's workshop and watch. The potter is working clay on a wheel. When the vessel is flawed, he does not discard it — he collapses it back into itself and starts again, reshaping it according to what he intends. God then says plainly: this is what I do with people. The image is physical and patient. Clay does not resist and argue. It yields. The second text is Psalm fifty-one, written by King David after a sequence of serious moral failures. It is one of the most direct prayers of repentance in the biblical record — not a performance of contrition, but a raw request: create in me something clean that I cannot create in myself. Both texts locate transformation not in human effort but in divine work. The person prays. God shapes.

## Reflection

If you have ever wanted to change something about yourself — genuinely wanted it — and still found yourself unable to, what did that inability tell you about where real change comes from?

## For Worship Leaders

"Change Me, Mold Me" works well in a context of confession or preparation for prayer — moments where the congregation is invited to slow down and be honest rather than celebratory. Its unresolved posture makes it most effective before a time of silence or spoken prayer rather than as a closing anthem. The restraint in the arrangement also makes it adaptable to smaller acoustic settings where simpler instrumentation will carry the weight without loss.
