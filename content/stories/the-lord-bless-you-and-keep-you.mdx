# The Lord Bless You

## The Song

There is a kind of exhaustion that prayer can produce — not because prayer is wrong, but because it is possible to approach God like a vending machine, feeding requests into the slot and waiting anxiously for the return. This song was written during a season shaped by that exhaustion. Anxiety had become a steady background hum, and the spiritual habit of petition — good in itself — had quietly curdled into striving. Every prayer felt like another attempt to earn an answer.

What broke that pattern was sitting with Numbers 6:24–26, a passage where God instructs Moses to have Aaron speak a specific blessing over the people of Israel. The text is not a prayer. It is a declaration. The priest does not ask God to consider blessing the people; he speaks the blessing as something already determined, already in motion. That distinction stopped me cold. There is a mode of receiving that is entirely separate from asking, and this song was written to inhabit that mode.

The goal was to write something that would function as a benediction — words spoken over the listener rather than words spoken toward God on the listener's behalf. Not a petition dressed in gentle language, but an actual pause. A place on the album where striving is not rewarded and is not required. You are allowed to simply stop here and receive.

## The Sound

The arrangement was built around restraint. Every instinct to add — another layer, a dynamic swell, a moment of harmonic tension that resolves into something satisfying — was resisted. What remains is minimal: slow, sustained phrasing with generous space between melodic movements. The pacing is unhurried almost to the point of discomfort for a listener conditioned to expect momentum. That discomfort is intentional. Stillness can feel unfamiliar.

The music avoids the conventional push and pull of tension and resolution because a blessing does not operate on those terms. It does not build toward a climax you have to earn. The harmonic choices stay open and unresolved in the traditional sense — not because the music is uncertain, but because what is being offered does not need to be proven or argued. It rests. The hope was that the sound itself would give the listener permission to do the same.

## The Scripture

Numbers 6:24–26 is one of the oldest recorded blessings in human history, and its structure is worth noticing. It reads: *The Lord bless you and keep you; the Lord make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the Lord turn his face toward you and give you peace.* Each line moves closer — from keeping, to shining, to turning toward. The image is of a face orienting itself in your direction with full attention and warmth. The word translated as "peace" here is the Hebrew *shalom*, which carries more weight than the English equivalent. It means wholeness, completeness, the absence of disorder. This is not the peace of a quiet room. It is the peace of something being set right. Psalm 91 and Psalm 57:1 run alongside this — both written from positions of real fear, both arriving at the same conclusion: that shelter exists, and that it is not earned by being afraid in the right way.

## Reflection

The distinction between receiving and achieving is easy to agree with and difficult to practice. If you have ever found that spiritual life — or life in general — has collapsed into a series of performances you are never quite finishing, this song was written with that specific weight in mind.

What would it mean for you to receive something good without first deciding whether you deserved it?

## For Worship Leaders

"The Lord Bless You" works well as a benediction or a closing moment in a service — spoken or sung over a congregation as they prepare to leave rather than as a call to further action. Its minimal arrangement leaves room for a spoken word, a moment of quiet, or a simple instrumental extension without crowding the space. The song asks nothing of the room except willingness to receive, which makes it particularly well-suited for services that have already carried significant weight.
